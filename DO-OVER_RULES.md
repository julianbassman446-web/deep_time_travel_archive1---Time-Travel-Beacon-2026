TEMPORAL DO-OVER RULES

Proposed Safety, Consent, and Non-Interference Framework

PURPOSE AND RESEARCH STATUS

This document establishes proposed principles for evaluating a possible future backward-time-travel intervention, referred to in this archive as a "do-over."

The framework is intended for serious scientific, legal, ethical, and engineering investigation. Its requirements have not been tested as an operational temporal system.

The purpose is to investigate whether a limited personal do-over could ever be considered without creating unacceptable harm to other people, communities, or historical events.

A request for a do-over does not establish that backward time travel is possible or that an intervention should be authorized.

No intervention may proceed without demonstrated scientific feasibility and the required independent approvals.

RULE 1: NON-INTERFERENCE BY DEFAULT

Backward-time intervention must be prohibited by default unless a future, scientifically demonstrated process establishes that a specific intervention can be considered safely and lawfully.

The following principles apply:

No person has an automatic right to alter historical events.

No intervention should proceed merely because an individual requests it.

The protection of other people's lives, rights, safety, and autonomy must be considered.

Any proposed intervention must be evaluated for direct and indirect consequences.

Uncertainty about possible harm must be treated as a reason for caution.

A proposed exception would require scientific evidence, legal authorization, independent ethical review, and appropriate informed consent.

The absence of a detected risk must not be treated as proof that no risk exists.

RULE 2: PROTECTION OF IMPORTANT HISTORICAL EVENTS

Any proposed do-over must be assessed for its potential effects on important historical events and the people connected to them.

The assessment should consider:

Whether the intervention could cause harm to another person.

Whether it could change significant events in ways that create unacceptable consequences.

Whether it could interfere with another person's rights, choices, or opportunities.

Whether the requested change could produce effects beyond the original objective.

Whether the proposed outcome can be assessed with sufficient scientific confidence.

Personal milestones and meaningful life experiences should be treated with care and respect.

However, no event should be declared scientifically or morally untouchable merely through an automated system. Decisions must be based on transparent legal, ethical, and safety standards.

The system must not assume that it can perfectly predict every consequence of changing an event.

RULE 3: LIMITED SCOPE AND PROTECTION OF OTHERS

The original intention of a limited personal do-over is retained.

Any future proposal would need to define its authorized scope before consideration.

The intended scope would be limited to the stated personal objective and the minimum necessary circumstances surrounding it.

However, a personal intervention cannot automatically be assumed to affect only the requester and people immediately nearby.

Possible indirect consequences must also be considered.

The assessment must include:

Directly affected individuals.

People whose circumstances could materially change.

Potential effects on communities and wider historical events.

Privacy and consent requirements.

The possibility of unpredictable consequences.

No private information may be accessed simply because someone appears on a traveler's personal Known List.

The Known List, if used in a future research model, must not override the rights, privacy, or autonomy of its members.

RULE 4: INFORMED CONSENT AND PERSONAL AUTONOMY

Any future research involving human participants must respect informed consent.

The following protections are proposed:

Participants must understand the purpose and potential risks of the research.

Personal information must be limited to the approved purpose.

No person may be subjected to unauthorized psychological, biometric, or neural monitoring.

Consent must not be assumed from silence or previous participation.

Participants must have a defined process for withdrawing consent.

No automated system may independently override a person's legal rights or personal autonomy.

A requester's consent does not substitute for the rights and protections owed to other affected individuals.

RULE 5: SCIENTIFIC AND LEGAL AUTHORIZATION

A do-over must not be treated as an available operation until the underlying technology has been scientifically demonstrated and independently evaluated.

Any future authorization process would require:

Evidence that the proposed temporal process is physically and technically possible.

Independent scientific assessment.

A documented safety and causal-impact evaluation.

Applicable government and legal authorization.

Independent ethical and privacy review.

Appropriate informed consent.

A defined procedure for stopping or suspending an unsafe operation.

No traveler, researcher, government agency, or automated system should have unrestricted authority to authorize an intervention.

RULE 6: PROHIBITED EXPLOITATION

Any future temporal intervention framework must prohibit the use of temporal technology for unfair personal or financial advantage.

Examples include:

Manipulating lottery outcomes.

Obtaining gambling advantages through historical knowledge.

Financial manipulation using information unavailable to others.

Deliberately exploiting or harming other people.

Circumventing legal protections.

Unauthorized interference with protected institutions or events.

These restrictions would apply equally to the original requester and all other participants.

RULE 7: SAFETY FAILURE AND WITHDRAWAL

If a proposed intervention cannot meet its scientific, legal, ethical, privacy, or safety requirements, it must not proceed.

A future system should provide a documented process for:

Rejecting an unsafe proposal.

Suspending an assessment when critical information is missing.

Reviewing new evidence.

Reporting safety concerns.

Withdrawing authorization when necessary.

Preserving appropriate accountability records.

A system must not claim that an intervention has zero risk or guarantee that all consequences can be reversed.

RULE 8: NO GUARANTEED OUTCOME

A do-over request is a proposed objective, not a guarantee of a particular result.

Future researchers must distinguish between:

The original event.

The requested alternative outcome.

The predicted consequences.

The scientifically demonstrated results, if any.

The possibility of alternate outcomes, historical convergence, or changes in causal relationships remains a research question.

No person should be promised that a proposed intervention will restore a previous life circumstance or produce an exact alternative history.

FINAL DIRECTIVE

The purpose of these rules is to preserve the possibility of responsible future research while establishing strong protections against misuse.

The original goal of a limited personal do-over is retained, but it must never take priority over the safety, rights, privacy, and autonomy of other people.

Non-interference remains the default.

Any future exception would require demonstrated scientific feasibility, independent review, informed consent, legal authorization, and an acceptable assessment of potential consequences.

A requested do-over is not permission to change history. It is a research objective that would first have to satisfy the highest applicable standards of safety, accountability, and respect for others.

Purpose and Status

These rules describe a proposed framework for evaluating and regulating a possible human do-over through backward time travel.

The framework assumes that sufficiently advanced time-travel technology and a Temporal Geographic Information System (Temporal GIS) may eventually exist. Neither capability has been demonstrated. These rules are proposed requirements for future research, engineering, legal oversight, and ethical review—not a description of an existing operational system.

The central objective is to permit a carefully limited do-over only if its effects can be responsibly evaluated and the rights, memories, safety, and well-being of everyone affected can be protected.

Rule 1 — Mandatory Temporal GIS Approval

No do-over may proceed without approval from the proposed Temporal GIS safety and cause-and-effect assessment system.

The system must evaluate the requested change, its intended scope, and its possible consequences before authorization.

Approval must also require appropriate legal authorization, independent ethical oversight, and evidence that the necessary technology can perform the assessment reliably.

A personal request does not, by itself, authorize a temporal intervention.

Rule 2 — Privacy-Protected Cause-and-Effect Analysis

A sufficiently advanced Temporal GIS may need to analyze extensive information to model the causal relationships surrounding a proposed change.

Its purpose is to determine how an adjustment might affect the requester and other people, including indirect consequences that may not be immediately apparent.

However:

The system must not provide the requester with access to private information they did not already know.

It must not disclose confidential conversations, private activities, protected records, or other previously unknown personal information.

It must not provide unrestricted surveillance access, camera access, or general-purpose monitoring.

Information processing must be restricted to what is legally authorized and necessary for the approved safety assessment.

Privacy protections must apply to the requester and everyone else.

The system should provide only the minimum necessary safety findings, rather than revealing the underlying private information.

The proposed system would need privacy-preserving technical methods, strict access controls, and independent legal oversight. Its ability to analyze information must never be treated as permission to disclose that information.

Rule 3 — Twenty-Year Historical Limit

A do-over must not reach more than twenty years into the past, measured from the date the time-machine request is made.

There is also a specific initial historical boundary:

If the request for a time machine is made before August 14, 2031, the intervention must not go earlier than August 14, 2011.

For requests made on or after August 14, 2031, the rolling twenty-year limit applies.

The system must verify the request date and the proposed destination date before approval. This is a maximum permitted window, not an automatic entitlement to travel to any date within it.

Rule 4 — Limited Adjustments to Wrinkles in Time

The purpose of a do-over is to adjust one or more specifically identified wrinkles in time, rather than unnecessarily rewrite a person's entire life or the lives of others.

The proposed intervention must be limited to the intended issue and must be assessed for direct and indirect effects.

The goal is to preserve everything that does not need to change.

A proposed adjustment must not unnecessarily remove:

Other people's non-regrettable memories.

Experiences that contributed to those memories.

Relationships, opportunities, or meaningful events that arose from those experiences.

Events that were not part of the intended correction.

The system must assess whether the intended adjustment could cause unrelated changes. If it cannot establish sufficiently reliable safeguards against unacceptable effects, the intervention must not proceed.

Rule 5 — Consent, Autonomy, and the Rights of Others

A do-over must respect the rights and autonomy of every person who could be affected.

The requester's consent does not replace another person's consent where that person's participation, information, or rights are involved.

The Known List, if used as part of the proposed assessment, may help identify relevant relationships and possible effects. Membership on that list must never authorize access to private information or give the requester control over another person's choices.

No person should be subjected to unauthorized psychological monitoring, memory extraction, or neural-interface access.

Rule 6 — Best Interests and Prevention of Harm

The objective must be to achieve the best reasonably assessable outcome for everyone affected, not simply to improve the requester's circumstances at another person's expense.

The system must consider:

Whether the proposed change could harm someone else.

Whether it could remove an important opportunity or meaningful experience.

Whether it could create new problems or transfer existing consequences to another person.

Whether the intended benefit can be achieved without unacceptable collateral effects.

The goal is to avoid leaving anyone to bear unintended or preventable consequences of the intervention.

No system may honestly guarantee that every consequence can be predicted or eliminated. If unacceptable harm cannot be ruled out to the required standard, the proposed do-over must be rejected or postponed.

Rule 7 — Prohibition of Exploitation

A do-over must not be used to exploit foreknowledge or manipulate outcomes for unfair personal advantage.

Prohibited purposes include:

Rigging lotteries or gambling outcomes.

Manipulating financial markets or private transactions.

Obtaining unfair advantages through information from the future.

Circumventing laws or legal accountability.

Deliberately harming, coercing, or controlling other people.

The framework is intended for carefully evaluated personal correction and harm prevention, not exploitation.

Rule 8 — Independent Review and Authorization

Before any intervention, the proposed process must undergo:

Scientific feasibility assessment.

Temporal GIS cause-and-effect evaluation.

Privacy and data-protection review.

Legal authorization.

Independent ethical review.

Consent and rights assessment.

Safety testing and a defined stopping procedure.

The system must not interpret a favorable assessment as proof that the intervention is risk-free.

Rule 9 — No Guaranteed Outcome

A request for a do-over is a proposed objective, not proof that backward time travel or a successful alteration is possible.

The technology must be independently demonstrated before any real-world intervention is considered.

The system must not promise that the past can be changed without consequences. Its responsibility is to identify, assess, and prevent unacceptable risks to the greatest scientifically demonstrated extent possible.

Final Directive

A Temporal GIS-approved do-over must be limited, privacy-protected, legally authorized, and evaluated for its effects on everyone involved.

The intended purpose is to correct a specific wrinkle or wrinkles in time while preserving other people's non-regrettable memories, experiences, autonomy, and meaningful parts of their lives.

The guiding principle is:

Make only the necessary adjustment, disclose no private information the requester did not already know, respect everyone's rights, and do not proceed unless the consequences can be responsibly assessed and unacceptable harm can be prevented to the required standard.
