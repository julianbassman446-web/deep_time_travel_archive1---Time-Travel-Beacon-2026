TEMPORAL DO-OVER RULES

Temporal GIS Authorization, Privacy, Cause-and-Effect, and Historical Preservation Framework

Purpose and Status

These rules describe a proposed framework for evaluating and regulating a possible human do-over through backward time travel.

The framework assumes that sufficiently advanced time-travel technology and a Temporal Geographic Information System (Temporal GIS) may eventually exist. Neither capability has been demonstrated. These rules are proposed requirements for future research, engineering, legal oversight, and ethical review—not a description of an existing operational system.

The central objective is to permit a carefully limited do-over only if its effects can be responsibly evaluated and the rights, memories, safety, and well-being of everyone affected can be protected.

Rule 1 — Mandatory Temporal GIS Approval

No do-over may proceed without approval from the proposed Temporal GIS safety and cause-and-effect assessment system.

The system must evaluate the requested change, its intended scope, and its possible consequences before authorization.

Approval must also require appropriate legal authorization, independent ethical oversight, and evidence that the necessary technology can perform the assessment reliably.

A personal request does not, by itself, authorize a temporal intervention.

Rule 2 — Privacy-Protected Cause-and-Effect Analysis

A sufficiently advanced Temporal GIS may need to analyze extensive information to model the causal relationships surrounding a proposed change.

Its purpose is to determine how an adjustment might affect the requester and other people, including indirect consequences that may not be immediately apparent.

However:

The system must not provide the requester with access to private information they did not already know.

It must not disclose confidential conversations, private activities, protected records, or other previously unknown personal information.

It must not provide unrestricted surveillance access, camera access, or general-purpose monitoring.

Information processing must be restricted to what is legally authorized and necessary for the approved safety assessment.

Privacy protections must apply to the requester and everyone else.

The system should provide only the minimum necessary safety findings, rather than revealing the underlying private information.

The proposed system would need privacy-preserving technical methods, strict access controls, and independent legal oversight. Its ability to analyze information must never be treated as permission to disclose that information.

Rule 3 — Twenty-Year Historical Limit

A do-over must not reach more than twenty years into the past, measured from the date the time-machine request is made.

There is also a specific initial historical boundary:

If the request for a time machine is made before August 14, 2031, the intervention must not go earlier than August 14, 2011.

For requests made on or after August 14, 2031, the rolling twenty-year limit applies.

The system must verify the request date and the proposed destination date before approval. This is a maximum permitted window, not an automatic entitlement to travel to any date within it.

Rule 4 — Limited Adjustments to Wrinkles in Time

The purpose of a do-over is to adjust one or more specifically identified wrinkles in time, rather than unnecessarily rewrite a person's entire life or the lives of others.

The proposed intervention must be limited to the intended issue and must be assessed for direct and indirect effects.

The goal is to preserve everything that does not need to change.

A proposed adjustment must not unnecessarily remove:

Other people's non-regrettable memories.

Experiences that contributed to those memories.

Relationships, opportunities, or meaningful events that arose from those experiences.

Events that were not part of the intended correction.

The system must assess whether the intended adjustment could cause unrelated changes. If it cannot establish sufficiently reliable safeguards against unacceptable effects, the intervention must not proceed.

Rule 5 — Consent, Autonomy, and the Rights of Others

A do-over must respect the rights and autonomy of every person who could be affected.

The requester's consent does not replace another person's consent where that person's participation, information, or rights are involved.

The Known List, if used as part of the proposed assessment, may help identify relevant relationships and possible effects. Membership on that list must never authorize access to private information or give the requester control over another person's choices.

No person should be subjected to unauthorized psychological monitoring, memory extraction, or neural-interface access.

Rule 6 — Best Interests and Prevention of Harm

The objective must be to achieve the best reasonably assessable outcome for everyone affected, not simply to improve the requester's circumstances at another person's expense.

The system must consider:

Whether the proposed change could harm someone else.

Whether it could remove an important opportunity or meaningful experience.

Whether it could create new problems or transfer existing consequences to another person.

Whether the intended benefit can be achieved without unacceptable collateral effects.

The goal is to avoid leaving anyone to bear unintended or preventable consequences of the intervention.

No system may honestly guarantee that every consequence can be predicted or eliminated. If unacceptable harm cannot be ruled out to the required standard, the proposed do-over must be rejected or postponed.

Rule 7 — Prohibition of Exploitation

A do-over must not be used to exploit foreknowledge or manipulate outcomes for unfair personal advantage.

Prohibited purposes include:

Rigging lotteries or gambling outcomes.

Manipulating financial markets or private transactions.

Obtaining unfair advantages through information from the future.

Circumventing laws or legal accountability.

Deliberately harming, coercing, or controlling other people.

The framework is intended for carefully evaluated personal correction and harm prevention, not exploitation.

Rule 8 — Independent Review and Authorization

Before any intervention, the proposed process must undergo:

Scientific feasibility assessment.

Temporal GIS cause-and-effect evaluation.

Privacy and data-protection review.

Legal authorization.

Independent ethical review.

Consent and rights assessment.

Safety testing and a defined stopping procedure.

The system must not interpret a favorable assessment as proof that the intervention is risk-free.

Rule 9 — No Guaranteed Outcome

A request for a do-over is a proposed objective, not proof that backward time travel or a successful alteration is possible.

The technology must be independently demonstrated before any real-world intervention is considered.

The system must not promise that the past can be changed without consequences. Its responsibility is to identify, assess, and prevent unacceptable risks to the greatest scientifically demonstrated extent possible.

Final Directive

A Temporal GIS-approved do-over must be limited, privacy-protected, legally authorized, and evaluated for its effects on everyone involved.

The intended purpose is to correct a specific wrinkle or wrinkles in time while preserving other people's non-regrettable memories, experiences, autonomy, and meaningful parts of their lives.

The guiding principle is:

Make only the necessary adjustment, disclose no private information the requester did not already know, respect everyone's rights, and do not proceed unless the consequences can be responsibly assessed and unacceptable harm can be prevented to the required standard.
